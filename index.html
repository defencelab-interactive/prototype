Case studies

six things we built, and what they changed.

Real engagements, clients anonymised. Each starts with the situation, not the technology — open one to see what we did about it.

Outgrown setup

A whole company on a shop-bought router

The office worked. Email arrived, files opened, Wi-Fi connected. The company had grown from a handful of people to a full operation and the network had grown with it, one device at a time, around a router bought from an electronics shop.

one flat network, one device, no record →×what we didclose

What that meant

Everything sat on one flat network — printer, cameras, laptops, personal phones and the systems holding company data could all reach each other. Nothing inspected what was leaving. Nothing recorded who had connected, or when. Updates happened when somebody remembered.

What we built

We replaced the platform rather than adding security on top of it, in stages, so the business kept working throughout. The office was separated into distinct networks. A redundant pair of firewalls replaced the single device. Every event went to central logging. Then every user network was left with no direct route to the internet at all — traffic runs through encrypted tunnels to exit infrastructure we operate in three countries, where controls are applied centrally.

What changed

The clearest proof came later: when the company opened a second office, it joined the same infrastructure instead of being designed from scratch. The first project became the template.

What this means for you. If your network was never designed, the useful first step isn't buying a firewall — it's finding out what you actually have.

Outgrown setup

Segmentation nobody notices

A large site with cameras, door controllers, printers, meeting-room screens, corporate laptops and a constant flow of visitors. Everyone agreed these shouldn't share one network.

12 networks · still in place →×what we didclose

What that meant

This is the most commonly abandoned security project there is, for one reason: when you separate them properly, the things people use every day stop working. Printers vanish. Screen sharing dies. Within a fortnight the segmentation is quietly reversed.

What we built

Twelve distinct networks, with traffic between them denied unless there was a reason for it — then the daily things made to work across those boundaries deliberately and narrowly. Printing worked but scanning didn't: scanning doesn't use the printing ports, and this printer model didn't use the standard scanning ports either. We identified the two non-standard ports it actually needed and opened exactly those two.

What changed

Staff print, scan and share their screen exactly as before. The result nobody praises you for is the one that matters: the segmentation is still in place months later, because it never became the reason somebody couldn't do their job.

What this means for you. If a previous attempt at separation was rolled back, that isn't a failure of the idea. Keeping it usable is most of the work.

Known gaps

Wi-Fi that knows who you are

One shared corporate Wi-Fi password — known by every current employee, every former employee, every contractor who had ever visited, and the personal phones of all of them. Changing it meant disrupting the whole company, so it never was.

offboarding: one action →×what we didclose

What that meant

Two consequences, and the second is worse. Access couldn't be removed when someone left. And the question “who was on the network last Tuesday” had no answer at all — the network could tell you a device had connected, not a person.

What we built

Wi-Fi authentication moved to the identity system the company already had, so joining the wireless network uses the same account as email. The same system authenticates remote access, with a second factor available. We also gave their IT lead and security lead their own read-only administrative accounts, so they can inspect any configuration, policy or log at any time without being able to change anything.

What changed

Offboarding became one action. Every connection is now attributable to a named account rather than a shared secret — which is what an auditor asks for and what an investigation depends on. And the client is no longer dependent on us to see their own environment.

What this means for you. If someone left last month, can you prove their access is gone? If the answer involves changing a password everybody uses, that's the gap.

Compliance

Guest Wi-Fi that survives an audit

A busy site with constant visitor traffic, and guest Wi-Fi that was either open enough to be a risk or awkward enough that reception ended up reading the password aloud across the lobby.

consent recorded · 30-day retention →×what we didclose

What that meant

The real exposure was elsewhere. Under European data protection rules, offering guest Wi-Fi means processing personal data. There was no recorded consent, no stated purpose, no retention position and no record of who had connected. Not a technical gap — a compliance gap.

What we built

A guest network where the compliance position comes first. Visitors see clear terms in both English and Spanish and must actively agree, then register with an email address. The notice states what is collected, why, that it is kept for 30 days, and that it is not shared. Access lasts 7 days per device. Returning guests inside that week aren't asked again. Certificates renew themselves so the portal never warns visitors the connection is untrusted.

What changed

The client can answer every question a data protection review asks: consent explicit and recorded, purpose stated, retention defined, each session attributable. And it costs the front desk nothing — nobody reads a password aloud any more.

What this means for you. Guest Wi-Fi is usually the cheapest compliance gap to close and the most commonly ignored. Often the fastest visible win in a wider programme.

Resilience

The quiet month when nothing happened

A smaller satellite office with no technical staff on site — the kind of location where a problem is discovered by people being unable to work, and getting someone there takes half a day.

0 users disconnected →×what we didclose

What that meant

A known software fault in the firewall was leaking memory, roughly 30 to 40 megabytes a day. After about 25 days memory use crossed 88 percent and the device entered a protective mode: it stops accepting new connections and passes traffic without inspection. The office becomes unreliable and less protected at the same time, with no obvious outward sign.

What we built

A properly configured redundant pair, with the second firewall fully synchronised rather than merely present. It was sitting at 43 percent, healthy, with an identical configuration, and it carried the office on 1,310 active connections and 380 Mbps while we worked — in daylight, instead of at three in the morning. Then we removed the problem permanently rather than waiting for the manufacturer's fix, with automatic responses built into the device itself.

What changed

Not one user was disconnected. Remote sessions stayed up, Wi-Fi kept working, nobody raised a ticket. The only visible effect was 20 to 30 seconds in which the administrative interface was unavailable. A fault that would have cost this site a morning every month became a solved problem.

What this means for you. Equipment rarely fails cleanly, it degrades. Protection needs a second device that is genuinely ready, monitoring that notices a trend, and a willingness to automate around a known fault.

Expansion

One console, every site

A single large site that had grown into 24 switches and around two dozen access points across several buildings, with a group plan to open further offices internationally.

24 switches · one console →×what we didclose

What that meant

Every change meant touching equipment individually. Every new location meant designing it again, sending an experienced engineer, and ending up with a site that differed slightly from all the others in ways nobody documented. That inconsistency is the hidden cost of expansion: no two sites are quite the same, so every problem is a new problem.

What we built

One management platform, hosted centrally, running the entire estate — all twenty-four switches, the access points, five wireless networks and twelve network segments from one place. Configuration is held as templates, with the site-specific details kept separately as variables. We also documented the environment as plain-language documents a new IT hire or a different provider could actually use.

What changed

Changes are made centrally and consistently, without travelling to a site. For the group's expansion the method is prepared: equipment is registered centrally before it ships, then finds its own configuration when powered on — a location operational within minutes, with no engineer on site.

What this means for you. If you plan to open locations, the decision that matters is made at the first site, not the third. A site built as a template costs a fraction of one built from scratch.
